---
title: "What does good technical judgment actually look like?"
date: "September 13, 2026"
dateISO: "2026-09-13"
---

Having been a professional software engineer for two and a half years now, I've started to think beyond pure technical execution and more about technical judgment. Even in the world of coding agents and AI, where the rate of building and shipping software is exponentially increasing, I believe technical judgment will be what separates good products and technologies from great ones, and ultimately good companies from those that become generational.

I believe we can narrow down what good technical judgment looks like into two core areas of building a technology company: 1) judgment in the context of product and 2) judgment in the context of resources. Ultimately, I don't think technical judgment exists in a vacuum — what is considered "good" depends on the product you are building and what your business is trying to accomplish.

The first vector on which to analyze good technical judgment is in the context of building product. As an engineer, your north star is always whether the product is getting better for customers. This applies even to domains like infrastructure, where you may be optimizing for speed or cost savings — the second-order effect is that you will allow the company to have a longer runway, which affords more time to build new things for your users. On the product side, good technical judgment comes down to solving all the hardest technical problems required to make a product as simple and delightful to use as possible. This ranges from building correct abstractions around integrations that make them reliable and extendable, to strong REST API fundamentals that make public-facing APIs simple to use, to choosing the right data models and databases that follow natural query patterns. For example, choosing the correct framework for handling async job queues, like SQS or Temporal, will affect how users interact with or get value from the product. It also often extends to knowing what to operationalize versus what to automate, especially in services and B2B products. For example, good engineers know when an operational process should be handled by the customer, when it should be handled by an internal operations person, and when they can entirely automate the process. One of the things that has stuck with me most is that the best technology companies know how to automate the long tail of problems — engineers with good technical judgment are the stewards of owning automation.

Technical judgment also has to be evaluated in the context of resources. It is unfair to compare what good technical judgment looks like at a company at the scale of Google versus at a scrappy 10-person startup trying to find product-market fit. Certain pieces of technical judgment apply universally, such as writing high-quality software with minimal bugs, building scalable processes, or following good software architecture practices. However, in the context of startups (which is where most of my experience lies), good technical judgment lies in understanding how to make the correct technical decisions with constrained resources, namely the number of engineers, money, and time. A few concrete examples of strong technical judgment in startups include choosing and extending the correct tech stack (using AWS Lambda when your product is nascent) or even the correct database scaling strategy (e.g., vertical scaling until maxing out DB size). The ability to make choices that enable product and engineering velocity is even more pertinent in the AI age, where there are constantly evolving frameworks and tools, and it's easy to rack up huge AI bills from using tons of tokens. An engineer with good judgment understands a few things: 1) how to design their codebase in the right way for agents to extend (e.g., implementing good frontend patterns from day one or organizing services in a clean way), 2) how to add boundaries for making agent-built software legible (skills, code review processes, providing agents the right context), and 3) how to choose the right set of tooling to provide their team, depending on the constraints mentioned above. At a large organization, there is value in having full teams working on an internal harness; at small organizations, the most effective setup could just be to give engineers access to Codex, Claude, and Cursor and put in the basic legwork to expose skills and MCPs across all three harnesses. Ultimately, with so much change happening with agentic coding, the best engineers will be the best at determining the simplest setups for their own teams and how to get the most productivity gains with those selected tools.

Good technical judgment extends far beyond writing a clean PR; rather, it represents an engineer's ability to make the correct technical decisions that lead to the best outcomes for their customers and their business. In a world where everyone has been given the superpower to write software, being able to make these decisions becomes more difficult and ambiguous and requires more intentionality. The best products and companies will be determined by the engineering teams that maintain strong technical judgment as a north star, rather than those that trade it for sheer output.
